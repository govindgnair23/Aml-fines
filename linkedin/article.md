# Fines don't work. So what would?

*What other industries can teach AML about deterrence*

---

Earlier this month the OCC fined American Express National Bank $350 million for failing to run a working AML programme. The failures spanned roughly eleven years, and about $13 billion in suspected trade-based money laundering went unreported.

Jim Richards made a sharp point about it: over that period the company earned about $70 billion. The fine is a rounding error. "FINES. DON'T. WORK."

I wanted to know whether the data backs that up. If it does, what *would* work? So I compared AML enforcement with how penalties are used in other regulated industries.

**[Image 1 – g1-context.png]**

Then, five days later, it happened again. On October 13, FinCEN fined Bank of America $1.2 billion. That total includes the OCC's largest-ever civil penalty, $750 million. The bank admitted *willfully* violating the Bank Secrecy Act and failing to report more than $5 billion in suspicious transactions on time, over $1 billion of it tied to national-security risks such as Chinese money-laundering networks.

A record OCC penalty sounds big, but it's about 4% of the $30.5 billion Bank of America earned in 2025. It also came 22 months after an OCC cease-and-desist order on the same programme. Two of the largest US banks were penalised in one week, each for roughly 3–4% of a year's profit, and neither faces an asset cap.

**[Image 2 – g1b-two-banks.png]**

## 1. AML fines aren't unusually small. They're just not the kind of sanction that changes behaviour.

I lined up landmark corporate penalties against the firm's net income for one year. They range from HSBC and TD to BP's Deepwater Horizon, Volkswagen's dieselgate, Pfizer, GSK and Boeing.

The surprise: big AML fines are about the same size as other big fines relative to profit. The median is roughly 20% of a year's earnings for AML and 28% for everything else. This month's two cases, AmEx at about 3% and Bank of America at about 4%, sit at the very bottom.

The cases that actually changed behaviour had something extra:
- **They cost more than a year's profit.** BP's total Deepwater bill was around $65 billion.
- **Or they restricted the business itself.** Wells Fargo's asset cap cost it billions in forgone profit, more than any of its fines. TD now has an asset cap and a guilty plea.

When American Express says the penalty was already reserved for and doesn't change its guidance, that tells you how it registers.

**[Image 3 – g2-penalty-vs-profit.png]**

## 2. The same failures, again and again

If fines deterred, we'd expect landmark cases to be followed by quiet. Instead:
- **HSBC, Standard Chartered, Deutsche Bank and Western Union** were all penalised again after their big settlements.
- **In the Netherlands,** ING's €775 million in 2018 was followed by ABN AMRO's €480 million in 2021.
- **In Australia,** CBA was followed by Westpac and then by the casinos.
- **In the US,** TD's failures ran from 2014 to 2023, entirely *after* HSBC's 2012 "landmark" case. AmEx's ran right through HSBC, Danske, Binance and TD. Bank of America's $1.2 billion penalty followed its own 2024 cease-and-desist order.

## 3. What the research actually says

Fifty years of deterrence research converge on a few points.

- **Certainty beats severity.** The perceived chance of being caught deters far more reliably than the size of the punishment (Nagin).
- **Fines alone don't deter companies.** A Campbell Collaboration systematic review of corporate crime found no significant company-level deterrent effect from punitive sanctions on their own. Combinations of measures did work.
- **Escalate predictably.** Regulators work best with an enforcement *pyramid*: persuasion at the bottom, escalating quickly to penalties, with a credible "big gun" at the top that is rarely used, such as licence loss (Ayres & Braithwaite).
- **Don't punish companies for looking.** If firms are liable for everything they uncover, they rationally look less. Liability should reward genuine monitoring and self-reporting (Arlen & Kraakman).
- **Reach the decision-makers.** When fines land on shareholders and the managers who made the calls have moved on with vested bonuses, the incentive never reaches the people deciding.

The measured effects from other industries are striking. **None of them comes from bigger fines:**
- One EPA fine cut water-pollution violations across a whole state by about two-thirds the following year.
- One OSHA press release did as much for compliance as about 210 extra inspections.
- Posting hygiene grades on restaurant doors in Los Angeles cut foodborne-illness hospitalisations by around 20%.

## 4. How regimes compare

I scored ten enforcement regimes against those principles: traffic, aviation safety, nuclear, food hygiene, tax, healthcare, workplace safety, cyber/data, aviation security and AML. It's a qualitative judgment, but the pattern is hard to miss.

**[Image 6 – g4-scorecard.png]**

AML comes last. The dividing line between the regimes that work and those that don't is **observability**:
- **Traffic** has cameras.
- **Aviation** has flight-data monitoring and non-punitive incident reporting.
- **Nuclear** has an inspector on site at every plant.
- **Tax** has third-party reporting. Income that employers report is misreported about 1% of the time; income nobody reports is misreported about 55% of the time. Same penalties, same people.

AML looks more like self-reported income. The bank is both the regulated party and the only source of evidence about itself. Regulators only find out what went wrong through a lookback, often a decade later.

The regimes that improved most didn't raise fines. They changed **how violations become visible**.

## 5. "But there are no victims"

There's a tempting conclusion that money laundering is victimless, so nobody notices. That's wrong. TD's case involved drug-trafficking proceeds. Westpac's unreported transfers included payments linked to child sexual exploitation.

The victims are real. But the harm is *indirect and hard to attribute to a specific bank's failure*. That's why AML enforcement penalises process failures ("suspected" activity unreported) rather than outcomes.

Switching to outcome-only enforcement wouldn't fix it either. It would need completed prosecutions linking a bank's failure to a crime, which are even rarer. Banks would rationally invest even less.

What we see today isn't banks under-spending. Compliance spend is enormous. It's banks spending on what examiners can see, such as policies, alert volumes and defensive SARs, rather than on whether any of it catches launderers.

## 6. What would work: install the speed camera

The idea I keep coming back to is **unannounced testing with synthetic transactions**. Regulators would run realistic laundering patterns through banks end to end and measure what gets caught.

There's precedent. Researchers posing as clients found that roughly half of company-formation agents would set up anonymous shells without proper ID. TSA red teams got mock weapons past screening in about 67 of 70 attempts. Regulators already mandate red-team testing for cyber resilience (CBEST, TIBER-EU).

Built into a system, it would look like this:

**[Image 7 – g6-system.png]**

1. **Measure.** Run frequent, unannounced, end-to-end tests. Score both detection and false positives, so banks aren't rewarded for drowning law enforcement in defensive SARs.
2. **Respond fast.** Apply modest, automatic consequences for poor scores, like a traffic ticket rather than a fine a decade later.
3. **Publish.** Release scores or rating bands. Publicity drove peer deterrence for OSHA and for restaurant hygiene.
4. **Align pay.** Defer executive pay beyond the detection lag and claw it back on supervisory findings. TD has just tied a US executive's award to AML remediation milestones, which shows it can be done.
5. **Escalate.** Asset caps, business limits and individual bans should follow automatically from repeated failure, not from negotiation.

It won't be easy:
- **Banks will learn to spot tests** (the Volkswagen problem), so tests must be indistinguishable from real traffic and constantly rotated.
- **It needs legal authority** to run synthetic identities and transactions.
- **Complex typologies are expensive to simulate,** trade-based laundering above all.
- **Any measured score gets gamed,** as the NHS's waiting-time targets showed.

But it's testable. A randomised pilot across a group of banks would tell us within a year whether it works. That's more than we can say about another decade of record fines.

## The bottom line

Jim is right that fines, as currently used, don't work. But the lesson from other industries isn't to make the fines bigger. It's to **make failure visible, respond quickly, reach the people who decide, and keep the big gun credible.**

We regulate traffic with cameras, not by waiting for crashes. It's time AML had its speed camera.
